# Data decisions

The cleaning rules and metric definitions I chose for this analysis, why, and how many rows, editors or edits each one affects. The notebooks, scripts and other documents refer to them by number.

| # | Decision | Reason | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Analyze Simple English Wikipedia (`simplewiki`) | English text, so examples are readable; small enough for a laptop (1 GB download) | Covers the whole history, 2001–2026 |
| 2 | **Bots:** an edit is a bot edit if its editor is flagged as a bot, by username or by the bot group, either at the time of the edit or today. Bot edits are excluded from the analysis | Using the flag at the time keeps the normal edits of 34 experienced editors who had a bot flag only for short automated tasks; adding today's flag catches 5 recently approved bots whose older edits have no flag | Removes 2,683,109 of 8,837,069 edits by registered accounts (30.4%), from 763 accounts. Bot edits stay in `raw_events` |
| 3 | **Logged-out editors:** anonymous (IP) editors and temporary accounts are grouped as "logged-out editors". They are left out of the newcomer analysis and kept as a separate group in community health. Edits with an unknown editor type are excluded | Anonymous editors have no ID, so they can't be followed over time. Temporary accounts (since September 2025) expire after 90 days, 68% make one edit, and nothing links them to a later registration; counting them as newcomers would inflate 2025–2026 cohorts. Logged-out editing is a fifth of all edits and a large share of what gets reverted | Newcomer analysis: 2,180,372 logged-out edits (19.8% of all edits) left out; newcomer cohorts start from the 152,625 registered accounts that ever edited. Unknown type: 6,546 edits excluded (4,549 with the editor hidden by administrators, 1,997 from 9 accounts with empty type columns) |
| 4 | **Automatically created accounts:** registered accounts count as newcomers whatever way they were created (by the person, by another user, automatically by the system, or unknown). The funnel's first step, "created an account", counts only accounts created by a person (self or another user); the other accounts enter the funnel at their first edit. Creation method is kept as a segment | An automatic account is created when someone logged in on another Wikimedia wiki visits this one, so it shows a visit, not a sign-up: only 5.8% of them ever edit. But those that edit are 60% of editing newcomers, so leaving them out would lose most newcomers | Newcomer cohorts: 151,908 human accounts that edited (bot edits removed), instead of 55,749 if only accounts created by a person counted. Funnel's first step: 167,008 accounts, instead of 1,761,982 if automatic accounts counted |
| 5 | **Newcomer:** a registered account's clock starts at its first human edit, in any part of the wiki. Newcomer cohorts are the years of that first edit, full years 2008–2025. Where the first edit was made is kept as a segment | For one automatic account in seven, the first edit came over a year after the account was created, so the creation date doesn't show when someone started. Counting only article edits would hide the 17.8% of newcomers who never edited an article. Before 2008 there were fewer than 5,000 newcomers a year, many with accounts rebuilt afterwards; from 2008 automatic accounts exist, so every cohort is made up the same way. 2026 is a partial year | 133,976 newcomers in 2008–2025, out of 151,908 in all years (10,165 before 2008 and 7,767 in 2026 left out). Clock at the first article edit would give 110,388, at account creation 135,214 |
| 6 | **Retained:** the main metric is a newcomer with at least one human edit, anywhere on the wiki, in months 3–6 after their first edit (days 60–180). The secondary, short-term metric is at least one edit in the second month (days 30–60) | Months 3–6 measure staying rather than finishing a first task, and give the largest retained group for the drivers analysis. The second month is known after 60 days, which suits an A/B test. All candidate definitions show the same trend over the cohort years, so the choice changes the level, not the story. Every window ends before day 300, the longest follow-up the December 2025 cohort allows | Main metric: 8,415 of 133,976 newcomers retained (6.3%). Secondary: 6,013 (4.5%). 5,442 newcomers are retained by the main metric without editing in the second month |
| 7 | **Reverted:** an edit counts as reverted if it was identity reverted (a later edit restored the page's earlier content) by someone other than the editor, within 30 days of the edit. The `mw-reverted` tag is not used | The tag barely exists before 2020, so it can't support 2008–2025 trends, and in 2021–2025 it marks only 64% of identity-reverted edits. A self-revert (20.5% of reverts) is a correction, not a rejection. 27.5% of reverts happen 60 or more days later, after the retention windows; with a 30-day limit, a revert of a first-day edit happens before the main retention window starts | Newcomers 2008–2025 with a first-day edit reverted: 32,878 of 133,976 (24.5%), instead of 38,808 counting every identity revert and 14,942 with the tag |
| 8 | **Activation funnel:** account created → 1st → 5th → 50th edit, each step within 180 days (the first edit counted from account creation, the 5th and 50th from the first edit) | Without a limit, recent cohorts would look worse only because they had less time. 180 days is the main retention metric's horizon and every 2008–2025 cohort can be followed that long | 148,478 accounts created by a person → 47,122 first edits; 133,976 newcomers → 27,220 5th edits → 2,803 50th edits. With no limit: 47,361 / 31,354 / 4,046; within 30 days: 46,612 / 24,273 / 1,914 |
| 9 | **Active editors:** per calendar month, an active editor has 5+ human edits and a very active editor 100+ | The thresholds Wikimedia uses in its own statistics, so results are comparable | Medians per month, 2008–2025: 1,089 editors, 254 active, 33 very active |
| 10 | **Retention curve:** the share of newcomers with at least one human edit in each 30-day month after their first edit, months 1–10 | Shows when newcomers leave, not only whether. Month 10 ends at day 300, the longest follow-up every cohort allows | 4.5% of newcomers edit in month 2, falling to 1.4% in month 10 |
| 11 | **Deleted pages:** edits on pages that were later deleted are kept in every count. An edit whose page was deleted within 30 days of the edit is a measured newcomer experience, like a reverted edit | Newcomers whose first article was deleted are part of the question; excluding their edits would remove 18,416 newcomers before asking why they left. Exclusion would also drop 67,615 edits on pages that were later restored, and make results depend on the snapshot date, since 22.1% of deletions come a year or more after the edit. The 30-day limit matches decision 7, so a deletion after a first-day edit comes before the main retention window | All 133,976 newcomers kept (115,560 if these edits were excluded). 25,385 newcomers (18.9%) had at least one first-day edit whose page was deleted within 30 days |
| 12 | **First-day experiences:** a newcomer's first experiences are measured over the 24 hours from their first human edit, on every part of the wiki: whether any of those edits was reverted (decision 7) and whether any edit's page was deleted within 30 days (decision 11). Articles only is shown once as a check | It is the window decisions 7 and 11 already use. The first edit session (edits less than an hour apart), used by Halfaker et al. (2013), gives nearly the same result. On this wiki, deleting a newcomer's profile page is often a real rejection, not housekeeping | Reverted: 32,878 newcomers, retained 3.0% vs 7.3% for the rest. Page deleted: 25,385, retained 3.4% vs 7.0%. First session instead: 31,603 and 24,359 (2.6% vs 7.4% for reverts). Articles only: 27,152 and 20,209 (2.9% vs 7.2%) |
| 13 | **Comparing drivers:** first-day experiences are compared in two ways: side-by-side tables within groups of newcomers with the same number of first-day edits, cohort period and creation method; and a logistic regression with all factors together (reverted, page deleted, number of first-day edits, cohort year, creation method, where the first edit was made) | More first-day edits mean more chances to be reverted and also go with staying, so a plain comparison mixes the two. The tables are easy to read; the regression checks every factor at once and is the method Halfaker et al. (2013) used, so the results can be compared | All 133,976 newcomers are in both. Hand-labeling newcomers as good-faith or not, as the paper did, is not part of this analysis |
| 14 | **Concentration of work:** per calendar year, registered editors are ranked by their human edits on every part of the wiki. Two measures: the share of edits made by the top 1% of editors (ties broken by account ID), and the number of editors who together make half of all edits | Calendar years match the cohorts and show a trend; a single 2008–2025 figure has no trend, and a month's top 1% is about 11 people, so its share jumps around. Every part of the wiki matches the newcomer and active editor definitions, and the top 1% make the same share of their edits in articles as everyone else (65% vs 66%). The number of people making half the edits is the plainest way to show how much the wiki depends on a few. Content added (bytes) is not used: vandalism, blanking and moved text distort it, and 13,193 edits have no size | Top 1%: 68–146 editors a year, making 73.2–84.6% of edits. Half of all edits: 7–26 editors a year. All of 2008–2025 as one period would give 85.1%; articles only 67.6–85.4% |
| 15 | **Mobile:** a newcomer started on mobile if their first human edit has the `mobile edit` tag (the mobile website and the apps together). It is a first-day factor for the cohorts 2014–2025 | The tag first appears in 2013, when mobile editing became possible for registered users; 2014 is the first full year. 97% of newcomers who start on mobile make all their first-day edits on mobile, so the first edit describes the first day. The apps are only 6% of mobile starts, too few to analyze apart | 25,531 of 97,170 newcomers of 2014–2025 started on mobile. Any first-day edit on mobile would give 26,007 (2015–2025), every first-day edit 24,083; cohorts 2015–2025 only, 24,931 of 92,040 |
| 16 | **A/B test design:** the test targets newcomers who have a first-day edit reverted, and the treatment is a friendly message sent when the revert happens. The primary metric is second-month retention (decision 6), with months 3–6 as a secondary metric. The test is sized to detect a rise of 1 percentage point, with a two-sided test at a 5% significance level and 80% power. Baselines come from the cohorts 2021–2025 | It tests notebook 02's main finding directly. 68% of first reverts come within an hour of the first edit, so the message reaches most newcomers while they are still on the site. For reverted newcomers the two retention metrics are almost equal (2.8% and 3.0%), so the second month gives the result 4 months sooner at about the same sample size. A rise of 2 points (72% relative) is too much to expect from one message; 0.5 points would take about 15 years | 2,566 reverted newcomers a year (12,828 in 2021–2025). The test needs 4,945 per group, 9,890 in all: 46 months of enrolment and 48 months to the result. Reverted or page deleted would take 31 months, all newcomers 20 months (with a welcome message instead); +2 points 15 months; 10% significance 38 months |
| 17 | **Dashboard:** a Streamlit dashboard deployed on Streamlit Community Cloud. It reads small summary tables (counts by year and group, with no account IDs) built from `wiki.duckdb` by a script and committed to git, an exception to the rule against committing data files. Filters: cohort years, creation method (signed up here, automatic, other) and the device of the first edit (2014–2025). Rates based on fewer than 50 newcomers are hidden | The 2.6 GB database can't go to GitHub or a free host, and a public link is what a hiring manager will open. Summary tables hold no information about any single editor beyond what the public notebooks show. First-day experiences stay out of the filters because the dashboard shows them on their own page. A rate from 50 newcomers can be off by about ±6 points by chance | The main summary table has 89 rows (cohort year × creation method × device), 12 of them under 10 newcomers. 21 groups (244 newcomers) are under 50 and are hidden when shown alone. Year only would give 18 rows; adding first-day experiences 1,136 rows, 657 of them under 50 |
